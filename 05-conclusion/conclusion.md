# Conclusion

This cross-sectional study of 788 community-dwelling older adults from NHANES cycle C (2003–2004) examined associations between comorbidity burden, dietary micronutrient intake (vitamin B12, folate), and objective balance performance measured via the modified Romberg test. The primary findings were null: neither comorbidity burden nor dietary micronutrient intake demonstrated significant associations with balance performance, and no significant interaction effects were observed. Age emerged as the only significant predictor, with older age associated with worse balance outcomes.

## Key Takeaways

**1. Age dominates balance performance in community-dwelling older adults.**
The strong, consistent association between chronological age and balance impairment suggests that aging-related physiological changes—encompassing declines in proprioception, vestibular function, muscle strength, and reaction time—may be the primary driver of balance decline in relatively healthy older populations. Clinical assessment and fall prevention efforts should prioritize age-stratified risk stratification, recognizing that advancing age itself signals elevated fall risk regardless of comorbidity profile or apparent nutritional adequacy.

**2. Dietary intake estimates may inadequately capture micronutrient-related balance risk.**
The absence of associations between dietary B12 and folate intake and balance performance contrasts with literature linking serum biomarkers of these micronutrients to physical function. This discrepancy underscores a critical methodological distinction: dietary recall data reflects ingestion, whereas biomarkers reflect absorption, metabolism, and tissue distribution. In older adults—who frequently experience gastric atrophy, medication-induced malabsorption, and variable bioavailability—dietary intake may be a poor proxy for biological micronutrient status. Future research and clinical practice should prioritize biomarker assessment when evaluating micronutrient-related functional risk.

**3. Comorbidity count may be less relevant to balance than medication burden or specific conditions.**
The null finding for comorbidity burden (count of diabetes, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia) diverges from literature documenting polypharmacy-associated balance impairment. This suggests that medication count, disease severity, or specific conditions with direct neurological or vestibular impact (e.g., peripheral neuropathy, Parkinson's disease) may be more salient predictors of balance than cardiometabolic comorbidity counts alone. Comprehensive medication review and deprescribing of potentially inappropriate medications may be more impactful for fall prevention than disease management in isolation.

**4. Survivorship bias substantially constrains balance research in older populations.**
The analytic sample represents only 45% of age-eligible participants with balance exam data, excluding adults unable to complete the standardized examination due to severe impairment, frailty, or safety concerns. This "healthy participant" bias likely attenuated associations and limits generalizability to the most vulnerable older adults. Recognition of this selection effect is essential when interpreting balance research and developing inclusive fall prevention strategies.

**5. Data limitations constrain causal inference and exposure assessment.**
The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, while the unavailability of vitamin D data, supplement use information, and serum biomarkers limited the ability to evaluate the exposures most relevant to balance per prior literature. These limitations highlight the importance of comprehensive data collection in population health surveys and the need for cautious interpretation of null findings when key variables are unavailable.

## Clinical Implications

Despite null findings for the primary exposures, this study supports several evidence-based clinical recommendations:

- **Age-based fall risk assessment**: Clinicians should prioritize fall risk screening for all older adults, particularly those aged 75+, recognizing that age itself is a robust predictor of balance impairment and fall risk.
- **Comprehensive medication review**: Rather than focusing solely on disease counts, clinicians should conduct regular medication reviews targeting potentially inappropriate medications (sedatives, anticholinergics, antihypertensives) that directly impair balance and cognition.
- **Biomarker-guided micronutrient assessment**: When clinical suspicion for deficiency exists (e.g., neuropathy, cognitive changes, macrocytic anemia), serum testing for B12, folate, and vitamin D should guide supplementation decisions rather than dietary history alone.
- **Inclusive care for the most vulnerable**: Alternative assessment strategies for older adults unable to complete standardized balance testing—such as home-based evaluation or caregiver proxy report—are needed to ensure fall prevention reaches those at highest risk.

## Contribution to the Literature

This study contributes to the literature by:

1. Providing a rigorously conducted, survey-weighted analysis of comorbidity-micronutrient-balance relationships in a nationally representative U.S. older adult sample.
2. Highlighting the methodological limitations of dietary intake-based micronutrient assessment in older populations and the importance of biomarker validation.
3. Demonstrating the predominance of age as a predictor of balance performance, potentially overshadowing comorbidity and dietary intake effects in community-dwelling samples.
4. Identifying survivorship bias as a critical limitation in performance-based balance research, with implications for study design and interpretation.
5. Informing future research priorities, including the need for longitudinal designs, biomarker-based exposure assessment, and inclusive sampling strategies.

## Final Remarks

The null findings of this study do not negate the importance of comorbidity management or micronutrient adequacy in older adults. Rather, they underscore the complexity of balance as a multifactorial outcome influenced by aging physiology, neurological function, medication effects, and environmental factors that may not be fully captured by comorbidity counts or dietary intake estimates. The predominance of age as a predictor reinforces that balance decline is an inherent feature of aging, requiring proactive, age-stratified prevention efforts.

Moving forward, research incorporating serum biomarkers, comprehensive medication assessment, longitudinal designs, and inclusive sampling will be essential to disentangle the contributions of disease burden, nutritional status, and aging itself to balance impairment. Until such evidence is available, clinicians should maintain a holistic approach to fall prevention—addressing modifiable risk factors including medication optimization, environmental modification, and strength/balance exercise—while recognizing that advancing age remains the most potent predictor of balance decline and fall risk in community-dwelling older adults.

---

**Summary Statement:** In this nationally representative sample of 788 U.S. older adults, balance performance was primarily determined by age, with no significant independent or interactive effects of comorbidity burden or dietary micronutrient intake (B12, folate). These null findings highlight methodological challenges in assessing micronutrient-balance relationships using dietary intake data and underscore the need for biomarker-based, longitudinal research in diverse older adult populations. Fall prevention efforts should prioritize age-stratified risk assessment, comprehensive medication review, and inclusive strategies that reach the most vulnerable older adults.
